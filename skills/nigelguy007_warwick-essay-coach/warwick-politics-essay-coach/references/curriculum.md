## Core modules

### PO107 Introduction to Politics (30 credits, full year)

**Assessment:** one 2,000-word assessed essay (40%) and a 2-hour online exam (60%). Must pass both.

**Indicative topics:** What is Politics?; Methods and Theoretical Approaches; Actors, Institutions & Ideas; Power and Authority; The State; Democracy & Legitimacy; Authoritarianism; Representation and Participation; Political Parties & Pressure Groups; Ideology; End of Ideology, Post-Truth, Populism, Media and the Crisis of Politics; Political Change and Revolution; Domestic and International Security; Identity Politics; Environment, Climate Change, Sustainable Development; Political Economy, Development and Social Welfare; Depoliticisation.

**Learning outcomes the essay is judged against:**
- Identify and critically discuss main theoretical perspectives and debates in political science.
- A knowledge base in political theory, processes, institutions and political change.
- Write appropriately constructed essays in a critical and analytical fashion.
- Independent study and communication skills.

**What markers look for in a PO107 essay:**
- Concepts defined and contested, not assumed: "power" (e.g. Dahl, Bachrach and Baratz, Lukes' three faces; Weber on authority and legitimacy), "the state" (Weber's definition and its critics), "democracy" (procedural vs substantive), "ideology".
- Awareness that the approach (behaviouralism, rational choice, institutionalism, interpretivism, Marxist, feminist) shapes the answer.
- Theory applied to real cases, comparing countries where the question allows.
- Use of neighbouring disciplines (sociology, economics, history, geography) where it helps.

### PO131 World Politics (30 credits, 20 weeks)

**Assessment:** 100% coursework: Essay 1 (25%) and Essay 2 (75%). An essay plan gets checkbox feedback on proposed structure, indicative bibliography and strength of proposed argument. Feedback within 20 working days.

**Weekly topics (as taught 2025/26):**
1. Introduction: assumptions about world politics
2. Realism
3. Liberalism
4. Marxism
5. Feminism
6. (Reading week)
7. Constructivism
8. Postcolonialism
9. World Politics Before Europe
10. Empires and Sovereign States
11. Cold War 1945–1989
12. Emerging World Order
13. Globalisation
14. Production and World Politics
15. Money and World Politics
16. (Reading week)
17. Global Governance
18. Poverty and Development
19. Global Environmental Governance
20. International Human Rights

**Learning outcomes:** familiarity with the major characteristics of world politics as developed by IR scholars; history of post-1945 world politics; core concepts; contemporary scholarly literature; critical analysis of ideas about world politics in writing.

**What markers look for in a PO131 essay:**
- Theories used as lenses with their assumptions stated (key actors, structure, what drives behaviour), not as labels. Realism is a family (classical, structural/neorealist, offensive/defensive); so is liberalism (interdependence, institutionalism, democratic peace).
- Theories compared or tested against a case, with a judgement about which explains more and why.
- Critical perspectives (Marxism, feminism, constructivism, postcolonialism) taken seriously, including what they say mainstream theory leaves out. Enloe's "the personal is international" frames the module.
- Historical grounding: empire, sovereignty, the Cold War. Watch for Eurocentric assumptions the module asks students to question.
- Scholarly books and articles from the reading list, not only the textbook.

### Third core module: academic and research skills

The course page lists a Year 1 core module on independent learning, academic skills and research methods. Its code and assessment weren't on the public pages checked; ask for the brief if an essay is for this module. Judge such work mainly on argument, use of evidence, method and referencing.

## Year 1 expectations in general (FHEQ level 4)

- Answer the question directly and take a position.
- Show understanding of the key debates and use relevant theory.
- Use scholarly sources (books, journal articles), not mainly websites, news or lecture slides.
- Descriptive summary of readings is the usual thing holding marks down at this level; analysis and evaluation move them up.
- A first at Year 1 doesn't need original research, but it does need a clear, well-defended argument that engages critically with the literature.
